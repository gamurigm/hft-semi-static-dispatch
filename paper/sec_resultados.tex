\subsection{Statistical Validation (Bootstrapping)}
The traditional parametric analysis was replaced by a Bootstrap resampling ($B = 10\,000$ iterations with replacement). This empirical method confirmed the robustness of the differences, calculating the resampling p-value ($p_{\text{boot}}$) on the actual CPU time distribution (Table~\ref{tab:bootstrap}).

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\caption{Bootstrap Analysis Results ($B=10\,000$, empirical 95\% CI)}
\label{tab:bootstrap}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccl@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Comparison} & \textbf{95\% CI (ns)} & \textbf{$d$} & \textbf{$p_{\text{boot}}$} & \textbf{Verdict} \\ \midrule
\texttt{switch} vs \texttt{FastBranch}     & $[8.95,\ 9.55]$ & 1.82 & $<0.0001$ & Large \\
\texttt{switch} vs \texttt{FlexBranch}     & $[8.70,\ 9.25]$ & 1.69 & $<0.0001$ & Large \\
\texttt{FlexBranch} vs \texttt{FastBranch} & $[0.00,\ 0.55]$ & 0.08 & $0.2053$  & Small \\
\texttt{FastBranch} vs Control             & $[0.00,\ 0.67]$ & 0.20 & $0.0201$  & Small \\ \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The 95\% Bootstrap CI of the \texttt{FastBranch} speedup stabilized at $[2.90\times, 3.14\times]$. The architectural advantage is statistically significant and highly dominant compared to the random \texttt{switch}, yielding a net latency saving of $\sim11.8$ ns per operation.

\subsection{Negative Findings}\label{sec:negativos}

\subsubsection{Multi-threaded Cache Contention}
Performance collapsed to $0.45\times$ of the single-threaded case. The cause was \textit{false sharing} under the MESI protocol: each \texttt{fetch\_add} from a different core invalidates the cache line of the remaining ones, effectively serializing execution.

\subsubsection{Predictor vs. Semi-Static}
When the condition changes infrequently ($1/10^4$ operations), the BPU achieves a high hit rate. In this scenario, the predictable \texttt{if/else} outperformed \texttt{FastBranch} by $22.3\%$ ($30.4$ ms vs $37.2$ ms), confirming that the overhead of an indirect \texttt{call} exceeds that of a correctly predicted jump.
